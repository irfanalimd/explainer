Quicksort: Split around one number, then do the same on each side
level core · 3 stories · 2 knobs (How many numbers, Your own numbers (optional))

HOOK      Comparing every number with every other one is slow. How does quicksort sort with far
          fewer comparisons, and when does that fail?
GOAL      Sort a line of numbers by placing one number for good at a time, and skip comparing most
          pairs.
OBJECTIVES
  o-place: Predict the slot a pivot lands in from how many numbers in its group are no bigger than
           it.
  o-split: Perform one split by hand: say which numbers swap as the scan moves along.
  o-skip: Explain why two numbers on opposite sides of a placed pivot are never compared. (random)
  o-slow: Predict which inputs make this pivot rule slow, and compute their comparisons: every pair,
          n(n − 1)/2. (sorted, dups)
PICTURE   a line of people ordered by height
  person  one person in the line  =  one number in its slot
  height  their height  =  the number's value
  chosen  the person everyone is measured against  =  the pivot  (pivot)
  step    shorter people step left, taller ones right  =  a partition, one split  (partition)
  again   the same again inside each smaller group  =  recursion  (recursion)
  breaks  People can all step aside at once. The computer checks one number against the pivot at a
          time and moves numbers only by swapping two.
BELIEFS
  m-middle: “The pivot ends up in the middle of the line, so every split cuts the group in half.”
            The pivot lands right after the numbers no bigger than it, wherever that is: the front,
            the end or anywhere between.
  m-pairs: “Every pair gets compared at some point; quicksort only changes the order of the
           comparisons.” Once a pivot is placed, its two sides are sorted apart. A number on one
           side never meets a number on the other.
  m-sorted: “Numbers already in order are the easy case: there is nothing left to do.” With the last
            number as pivot, sorted input places one number per split and compares every pair: the
            slowest case.
  m-equal: “A copy of the pivot can't count as small, so it goes right or waits beside the pivot.”
           This split's test is “no bigger than the pivot”, so every copy goes left; only a
           three-way split keeps copies beside it.
WORDS
  pivot: (the chosen number) The number every other number in the group is compared with. Here it is
         the group's last number.
  partition: (one split) One pass that moves the numbers no bigger than the pivot to its left and
             the bigger ones right.
  recursion: (the same again, smaller) Solving each smaller piece with the same steps, until a piece
             has one number or none.
NUMBERS   comparisons * · swaps   (* in each pause's recap)

  35  ★ Sorted with 21 comparisons, where checking every pair takes 36.   ‹sorted.done›
        why       15 comparisons never happened: pairs split apart by a pivot never meet.
        look      a
        · cost    With random pivots quicksort expects 2(n + 1)H(n) − 4n comparisons, where H(n) = 1
                  + 1/2 + … + 1/n: 20.6 for 9 numbers and 32.7 for 12. For large n it grows like
                  1.39 n log2 n. This run made 21.
        · edge    The worst case is still every pair: McIlroy showed any deterministic pivot rule
                  has inputs that force it.
        · real    Production sorts build on this. Java sorts `int[]` with dual-pivot quicksort (two
                  pivots, three groups); Rust's `sort_unstable` is ipnsort, a quicksort that falls
                  back to heapsort so it is never quadratic.
        · source  McIlroy 1999; Java SE 21, Arrays.sort; Rust, slice::sort_unstable

   RECAP
     1. Pick one number, the pivot: numbers no bigger go left, bigger ones right.
     2. The pivot then sits in its final slot and never moves again.
     3. Each side is split the same way, until every group has one number or none.
     4. The two sides are never compared, so even splits skip most pairs.
   TAKEAWAY  Each split puts one number in its final place and leaves two smaller groups that never
             need comparing with each other.

   1  ★ The simple way: 8 comparisons find the smallest, 7 the next, and so on.   ‹line.simple›
        why       Every number gets checked against every other: 36 comparisons for 9 numbers.
        look      a[4]
        · cost    Every pair once: n(n − 1)/2 = 9 × 8 / 2 = 36. Double the numbers and the work

        · edge    Not every simple sort is this slow on every input: insertion sort checks numbers

   ▸ Chapter 2 of 5 · Split around one
     about     Pick one number, the pivot, like the chosen person in the line. Numbers smaller than
               it or equal to it go to its left, bigger ones to its right. Slots are numbered from
               0, as in the code.
     so far    The simple way checks every pair, ties or not: 36 comparisons.
     now       Choose one number, and move each other number left or right, even its copies.
     done when Every number left of `i` is no bigger than the chosen one.
   2  ★ 3, the last number, is the pivot.   ‹split.pivot›
        why       Every other number will be compared with it once, then sent left or right.
        look      a[8]
        picture   the person everyone is measured against = the pivot
        · how     The pivot is `a[hi]`, the last slot of the group. CLRS uses this rule: it costs
                  nothing to pick, but it never looks at the numbers.
        · code    `pivot = a[hi]` (line 9)
        · edge    If the last number is the biggest or the smallest, every other number lands on one
                  side, and the split places only one number.
        · source  Cormen et al. 2022, ch. 7
   3  One partition: `i` marks where small numbers go, `j` checks each in turn.   ‹split.pointers›
        why       The rule that always holds: nothing left of `i` is bigger than the pivot.
        look      a.i, a.j
        picture   shorter people step left, taller ones right = a partition, one split
        · how     CLRS starts `i` one slot early (`i = p − 1`) and adds 1 before each swap; this
                  code starts at `lo` and adds 1 after. The rule is the same: slots before `i` hold
                  the small side.
        · code    `i = lo` and `for j in range(lo, hi):` (lines 10–11)
        · real    This one-marker split is Lomuto's, made popular by Bentley and CLRS because it is
                  easy to prove. Hoare's original (1961) walks two markers toward each other from
                  both ends and swaps a pair on the wrong sides: fewer swaps, harder to get right.
        · source  Cormen et al. 2022, ch. 7; Hoare 1961; Hoare 1962
     PREDICT  3 is the same as the pivot, 3. Which side does it go to?
